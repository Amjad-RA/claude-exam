ഡൊമെയ്ൻ ഒന്ന്: പ്രോംപ്റ്റിങ്ങും ടാസ്ക് ചെയ്യലും (Prompting and Task Execution)

നമസ്കാരം! ഈ ഭാഗത്ത് നമ്മൾ പഠിക്കുന്നത്, ക്ലോഡിനോട് (Claude) ഒരു ജോലി എങ്ങനെ വ്യക്തമായി പറഞ്ഞുകൊടുക്കാം എന്നാണ്. അതായത്, ഒരു നല്ല പ്രോംപ്റ്റ് (prompt) എങ്ങനെ എഴുതാം എന്ന്. പരീക്ഷയിലെ ഏകദേശം പതിനാല് ശതമാനം (14%) ചോദ്യങ്ങൾ ഈ ഭാഗത്തുനിന്നാണ്. നാല് കാര്യങ്ങളാണ് നമ്മൾ നോക്കുന്നത്.

ഒന്ന്: പുതിയ ജോലിക്കാരനോട് എന്നപോലെ പറയുക (1.1 Effective prompts)

നിങ്ങളുടെ ഓഫീസിൽ ഇന്ന് ഒരാൾ പുതുതായി ജോലിക്ക് കയറി എന്ന് കരുതുക. നിങ്ങളുടെ സ്ഥാപനത്തെക്കുറിച്ചോ, ഈ ജോലി എന്തിനാണെന്നോ അയാൾക്ക് ഒന്നും അറിയില്ല. ക്ലോഡും അതുപോലെയാണ്. നിങ്ങൾ എഴുതിക്കൊടുക്കാത്തതെല്ലാം അതിന് ഊഹിക്കേണ്ടി വരും.

അതുകൊണ്ട് ഒരു നല്ല പ്രോംപ്റ്റിൽ അഞ്ച് കാര്യങ്ങൾ വേണം. ഒന്ന്, എന്താണ് ചെയ്യേണ്ടത്: ടാസ്ക് (task). രണ്ട്, ആർക്കുവേണ്ടി, എന്തിനുവേണ്ടി: ഓഡിയൻസ് (audience and goal). മൂന്ന്, ഉത്തരം ഏത് രൂപത്തിൽ വേണം, ടേബിളോ ലിസ്റ്റോ: ഫോർമാറ്റ് (format). നാല്, പരിധികൾ, എത്ര വാക്ക്, എന്തൊക്കെ ഒഴിവാക്കണം: കൺസ്ട്രെയിന്റ്സ് (constraints). അഞ്ച്, എത്ര ആഴത്തിൽ വേണം: ഡെപ്ത് (depth). ഈ അഞ്ചാമത്തേതാണ് ആളുകൾ ഏറ്റവും കൂടുതൽ മറക്കുന്നത്.

ചില ചെറിയ ടിപ്പുകൾ കൂടി. ഒരു നിയമം പറയുമ്പോൾ അതിന്റെ കാരണവും പറയുക; അപ്പോൾ ക്ലോഡിന് അത് മറ്റു സന്ദർഭങ്ങളിലും പ്രയോഗിക്കാൻ കഴിയും. നീളമുള്ള ഡോക്യുമെന്റ് പ്രോംപ്റ്റിന്റെ മുകളിൽ വെക്കുക, ചോദ്യം ഏറ്റവും അവസാനം. ഉദാഹരണം കൊടുക്കുമ്പോൾ ഒന്നിൽ തുടങ്ങുക; ഉത്തരം ശരിയാകുന്നില്ലെങ്കിൽ മാത്രം കൂട്ടുക. എന്ത് വേണ്ട എന്ന് പറയുന്നതിനേക്കാൾ നല്ലത്, എന്ത് വേണം എന്ന് പറയുന്നതാണ്.

രണ്ട്: വലിയ ജോലി ചെറിയ ഘട്ടങ്ങളാക്കുക (1.2 Task decomposition)

ഒരു വലിയ ജോലി ഒറ്റയടിക്ക് ചോദിക്കുന്നതിനു പകരം, അതിനെ ചെറിയ ഘട്ടങ്ങളായി മുറിക്കാം. ഇതിനെ പ്രോംപ്റ്റ് ചെയിനിങ് (prompt chaining) എന്നു പറയുന്നു. ഓരോ ഘട്ടവും ഒരു ചെറിയ ജോലി മാത്രം ചെയ്യുന്നു. അടുത്ത ഘട്ടത്തിലേക്ക് കൈമാറുന്നതിനു മുമ്പ് നമ്മൾ ഫലം പരിശോധിക്കുന്നു.

ഒരു കാര്യം ശ്രദ്ധിക്കുക: ചെയിനിങ് വേഗം കൂട്ടില്ല, കുറച്ചുകൂടി സമയമെടുക്കും. പക്ഷേ ഉത്തരം കൂടുതൽ കൃത്യമാകും. ആദ്യ ഘട്ടത്തിലെ ഒരു തെറ്റ് പരിശോധിക്കാതെ വിട്ടാൽ, അത് പിന്നീടുള്ള എല്ലാ ഘട്ടങ്ങളിലേക്കും പടരും.

മൂന്ന്: ഉത്തരം മോശമായാൽ, ആദ്യം കാരണം കണ്ടെത്തുക (1.3 Iterating prompts)

ആദ്യത്തെ ഉത്തരം ശരിയായില്ലെങ്കിൽ, അതേ ചോദ്യം വീണ്ടും ചോദിക്കരുത്; പ്രോംപ്റ്റ് വെറുതെ നീട്ടുകയുമരുത്. ആദ്യം പ്രശ്നം എന്താണെന്ന് കണ്ടെത്തുക. ചോദ്യം അവ്യക്തമായിരുന്നോ? ആവശ്യമായ വിവരം കൊടുക്കാൻ മറന്നോ? അതോ ജോലി ഒറ്റ പ്രോംപ്റ്റിന് വളരെ വലുതാണോ?

എന്നിട്ട് ഒരു സമയം ഒരു കാര്യം മാത്രം മാറ്റി, ഫലം പരിശോധിക്കുക. ഓർക്കാൻ എളുപ്പത്തിന്: കണ്ടെത്തുക, മാറ്റുക, പരിശോധിക്കുക (diagnose, change, test). ഒരു ഉദാഹരണത്തിൽ മാത്രം നന്നായി പ്രവർത്തിച്ച പ്രോംപ്റ്റ്, ടീമിന് മുഴുവൻ കൊടുക്കുന്നതിനു മുമ്പ്, പല തരം ഇൻപുട്ടുകളിൽ പരീക്ഷിച്ചു നോക്കണം.

നാല്: ജോലിയുടെ തരം അനുസരിച്ച് പ്രോംപ്റ്റ് (1.4 Task types)

അനാലിസിസ് (analysis) ആണെങ്കിൽ, വിവരം മതിയാകുന്നില്ലെങ്കിൽ അത് തുറന്നു പറയാൻ അനുവാദം കൊടുക്കുക. ഇല്ലെങ്കിൽ ക്ലോഡ് ഊഹിച്ച് വിടവ് നികത്തിയേക്കാം.

റിസർച്ച് (research) ആണെങ്കിൽ, പല സാധ്യതകളും ഒരുമിച്ച് പരിഗണിക്കാനും, വിവരങ്ങൾ പല സ്രോതസ്സുകളിൽ ഒത്തുനോക്കാനും പറയുക.

എഴുത്ത് ജോലിയാണെങ്കിൽ (drafting), നിങ്ങളുടെ മുൻകാല എഴുത്തുകളുടെ സാമ്പിളുകൾ കൊടുക്കുക. സൗഹൃദപരമായി എഴുതൂ എന്ന് മാത്രം പറഞ്ഞാൽ പോരാ; ശൈലി കാണിച്ചുകൊടുക്കണം.

ബ്രെയിൻസ്റ്റോമിങ് (brainstorming) ആണെങ്കിൽ, ആദ്യം ധാരാളം ആശയങ്ങൾ ചോദിക്കുക. അവയിൽ ഏതാണ് നല്ലത് എന്ന് വിലയിരുത്തുന്നത് പിന്നീട്, വേറൊരു ഘട്ടത്തിൽ.

പരീക്ഷയിലെ കെണികൾ (Exam traps)

ഒന്ന്: മാർക്ക്ഡൗൺ (markdown) വേണ്ട എന്ന് വലിയ അക്ഷരത്തിൽ ആവർത്തിച്ചാൽ പ്രശ്നം തീരും എന്ന് കരുതരുത്. ക്ലോഡ് പ്രോംപ്റ്റിന്റെ ശൈലി അനുകരിക്കും. പ്രോംപ്റ്റ് തന്നെ മാർക്ക്ഡൗൺ നിറഞ്ഞതാണെങ്കിൽ ഉത്തരവും അങ്ങനെയാകും. രണ്ട്: കൂടുതൽ ഉദാഹരണങ്ങൾ എപ്പോഴും നല്ലതല്ല. മൂന്ന്: ചെയിനിങ് ജോലി വേഗത്തിലാക്കില്ല, കൂടുതൽ കൃത്യമാക്കും.

ഓർത്തുവെക്കാൻ മൂന്ന് കാര്യങ്ങൾ (Recap)

ഒന്ന്, പുതിയ ജോലിക്കാരനോട് പറയുന്നതുപോലെ എല്ലാം എഴുതിക്കൊടുക്കുക. രണ്ട്, വലിയ ജോലി ഘട്ടങ്ങളാക്കി, ഓരോ ഘട്ടവും പരിശോധിക്കുക. മൂന്ന്, ഉത്തരം മോശമായാൽ ആദ്യം കാരണം കണ്ടെത്തുക, പിന്നെ ഒരു കാര്യം മാത്രം മാറ്റുക. അടുത്ത ഡൊമെയ്നിൽ കാണാം!

1.1 Effective prompts: brief, reason, source, example

Treat Claude like a newly hired contractor. It knows nothing about you, your organisation or why the task exists. A good test: if a colleague who doesn't know the task would be confused by the prompt, Claude will be too.

TaskA direct verb: Write, Analyze, Create
Audience and goalWho reads it, and what it is for
FormatTable, list, prose, fixed columns
ConstraintsWord count, page limit, what must not appear
DepthThe most often skipped. Ask for comprehensive if you want it.

💬 Give the reason

Attach the "why" to an instruction, for example "no ellipses, because a text-to-speech engine will read this". Claude can then generalise the rule to cases it doesn't name.

📄 Ground it in a source

  • Wrap each kind of content in its own tag: <instructions>, <context>, <input>
  • Put long documents at the top, above the question
  • For noisy documents, have Claude quote the relevant passages first
  • Say what to do if the answer isn't there: "say so" or "use null"

🧪 Show one example

Start one-shot and add more examples only if the output still misses. Claude copies the details of examples closely. Wrap them in <example> tags.

🪞 Format: say it positively, and match it

Write "use flowing prose paragraphs", not "don't use markdown". Claude mirrors the prompt's own style, so a prompt full of markdown pulls the answer towards markdown.

Exam trapAnswers like "put 'NO MARKDOWN' in bold or capitals", "add more examples" or "put the question first with long documents" are wrong. Use a positive instruction, a prompt written in the style you want, one example, and the document above the question.

1.2 Task decomposition

Prompt chaining
A fixed sequence of smaller prompts. Each does one job and works on the checked output of the step before.
Trade-off
It's slower, because there are more calls. You get accuracy and reliability, and every intermediate result can be inspected.

✓ Do

  • Give each part a definition of "done" you can test
  • Run independent parts in parallel; chain the parts that depend on each other
  • Check intermediate steps before later stages build on them
  • Pass extracted quotes and clauses forward
  • Examples: cluster tickets, write an FAQ per cluster, then merge. Extract key clauses, then compare them point by point.

✗ Don't

  • Split when one better prompt would do. Every extra stage adds coordination cost and another way to fail.
  • Review only the final output. An early error is inherited by every later stage.
  • Pass only summaries forward. They drop detail later stages need.

Exam trapA reworded retry or a much longer prompt gives you a different output, not a better one. Changing several things at once means you can't tell which change helped. A prompt tuned on one input may be overfitted to it, so test on varied inputs and edge cases before making it the team standard.

1.4 Prompting by task type

TaskTypical failureWhat the prompt must include
AnalysisInvented conclusionsThe evidence, the focus, and permission to say "not enough data"
ResearchUnfocused searching, or only confirming one theoryScope; competing hypotheses with confidence tracked; cross-check sources; self-critique
DraftingGeneric proseVoice samples from past work plus real facts; a draft, review, refine chain
BrainstormingA narrow set of ideasAsk for range and breadth, then evaluate in a separate step

If one request mixes several task types, split it so each part gets its own prompt.

Claude keeps returning markdown even though you asked for plain text. The prompt itself is full of bullets and bold. What's the fix?

Rewrite the prompt itself without markdown. Claude's output style mirrors the prompt's style.

Does prompt chaining make a task faster?

No. It adds calls, so latency goes up. The gain is accuracy and outputs you can inspect.

How many examples should you start with?

One (one-shot). Add more only if the output still misses.

Describing your tone as "warm but professional" isn't giving you on-brand drafts. What's missing?

Voice samples from past successful work, plus the facts only you know. Voice is easier to show than to describe.

A clinic assistant's summaries keep citing irrelevant details buried in long patient records. Which change best reduces this noise?
  1. Move the question above the records so Claude knows what to look for
  2. Add five more example summaries
  3. Ask Claude to quote the relevant passages in a tag first, then summarise from those quotes
  4. Tell it in bold to ignore irrelevant information
C. Quoting first grounds the answer in the relevant parts. Long documents belong above the question. More examples and bold negative instructions don't target the cause.
D2

Output Evaluation and Validation

21%
Highest-weighted domainOne rule runs through all six lessons: a confident, fluent or cited answer is not a verified answer. How sure Claude sounds is only loosely tied to how reliable it is.

2.1 Accuracy and completeness

🔎 Discernment

Judging what Claude returns before you act on it: are the claims true, and is everything that was asked delivered? Measure it against standards you wrote down in advance.

🎯 Triage

Check first the precise, niche or recent claims the decision rests on. That's where errors cluster and cost the most. Reading for things that "seem odd" doesn't work, because invented details look authoritative.

✅ Completeness

Turn the request into a checklist. "Six questions asked" means checking six answers are present. An accurate answer that skips a question still fails.

📊 Several dimensions

Score on correctness and relevance, tone and consistency. Well-defined criteria applied consistently keep the score reliable.

Description–Discernment loopWhat an evaluation finds feeds a sharper description in the next prompt. Evaluation isn't the last step.

2.2 Hallucination, inconsistency or bias?

KindWhat it looks likeExample
HallucinationInvented, with no basis in any sourceA citation to a journal article that doesn't exist; "I've sent the email" with no email tool connected
InconsistencyContradicts itself, its source or another run"Ships in 3 days", later "7 days", with no new information in between
BiasLeans one waySkewed defaults, missing perspectives, uneven framing
MiscalibrationConfidence the evidence doesn't supportAgreeing with whatever the user says, or hedging that adds no information

Exam trapClassify what you can observe. Two conflicting values in one chat is an inconsistency. Calling it a hallucination would need evidence about which one was invented. If two chats give different answers and no document grounds them, trust neither, because both were generated rather than recalled.

2.3 Fact-checking and validation

Prompt so it can be checkedAllow "I don't know"; ask for quotes and references
Decision-critical claims firstTriage by consequence
Open the original sourceThe citation only tells you where to look

Exam trap"Ask Claude which of its claims are likely wrong" means the source being tested grades itself. "Tell it to answer more authoritatively" only changes how a guess is presented. To reduce fabrication, explicitly allow Claude to say it can't verify something. With no tools, a specific stock price from yesterday is almost certainly invented.

2.4 When human review is required

Every output gets some review. The stakes, audience and content type decide who reviews it and how deeply. For high-stakes work, the reviewer must be qualified in the field and accountable, and must review before the output reaches anyone it could harm.

Use caseRequired reviewer
Next-step suggestions for patientsA licensed clinician approves each one
Contract risk summaries before signingA licensed attorney reviews the clauses and the full contract
Investment recommendations for clientsA licensed financial advisor checks suitability and compliance
Not substitutes for qualified reviewA second Claude instance or a self-check (that's still Claude checking Claude); a citation on every claim; a polished, confident tone; an "AI-generated" label; a quick read for tone by someone who isn't an expert.

2.5 Refining for the audience

✓ Specific revision requests

  • Name the change: "add the cost figures", "cut steps 3 and 4"
  • State the goal next to the instruction, so Claude follows the intent and not just the wording
  • Use a role, context and examples to set register and depth
  • Different audiences get separate versions

✗ Vague revision requests

  • "Make it better", "more compelling"
  • Repeating a tone or length instruction more firmly when the draft misses the point

2.6 Choosing the output format

The format follows the destination. A quick answer goes inline. A standalone piece someone will reuse goes in an artifact. Output a program will parse needs a schema-enforced structure.

Artifact: the four criteria

  1. Significant and self-contained, typically over 15 lines
  2. Likely to be edited, iterated on or reused outside the chat
  3. Stands on its own without the conversation
  4. You'll refer back to it later

SVG logos, Mermaid flowcharts and React components are all artifact types.

Structured data

  • Use JSON with a schema when a program will parse the output
  • Passing the schema guarantees types and fields, not correct values. Even enum casing can drift without an error.
  • For people, choose between prose and lists or tables; don't send them JSON.
  • Inline custom visuals are not kept by default. Save one as an artifact to keep it.
An answer has no false statements. Is it complete?

Not necessarily. Completeness is measured against the request. An accurate answer that skips a question still fails.

Claude says "I've emailed the client." Did it?

Only if an email tool is actually connected. Otherwise it's a hallucinated claim about its own capabilities.

Someone asks "What does ACID stand for?" Inline or artifact?

Inline. It's short, depends on the conversation, and nobody will edit or reuse it.

You have 1 hour and 20 claims to check. Where do you start?

With the few claims the decision actually rests on. Triage by consequence, not by order of appearance.

A team wants Claude-drafted credit explanations sent to applicants. They plan for a second Claude instance to review each one. Is that enough?
  1. Yes, a second instance with a different prompt catches the errors
  2. No. A qualified loan officer must review each one before it's sent, and AI involvement must be disclosed.
  3. Yes, if every claim includes a citation
  4. No. They should spot-check 10% of explanations afterwards.
B. Financial decisions are high-risk. A second instance is still Claude checking Claude, citations only point to sources, and sampling afterwards lets Claude effectively make the decision.

3.3 Matching the tier to cost, speed and quality

Set the quality barBased on that consequence
Choose the cheapest and fastest optionThat still clears the bar
Test on your own workCompare tiers side by side

Effort levels

  • low: most efficient, some capability lost (subagents, simple tasks)
  • medium: balanced; the default on Opus 5.5 and Haiku 5.5
  • high: spends as many tokens as the task needs; the default on Sonnet 5.5

If effort was lowered, restore it before moving up a tier.

Rules of thumb

  • Compare the cost per completed task, not the price per token. A stronger model may finish in fewer turns.
  • For high-volume or simple work, the strongest tier costs more, runs slower and adds nothing.
  • For a batch, separate the few items that need judgment; send the routine bulk to a cheap tier.
  • Fast mode is the same model, faster, at a higher price. It doesn't make the model smarter.
  • To decide on a downgrade, measure it on your work. Don't ask Claude to predict it.

4.3 Solution design, development and iteration

User voice a messy human need
Requirement what must be true
Technical spec with a definition of done
Prompt what Claude acts on

This is the Description Chain. If the result works but fails the user, a link earlier in the chain broke.

✓ Plan, verify, judge, refine

  • Have Claude plan before changing anything (plan mode)
  • Give it a check it can run itself
  • Use a separate verifier that tries to disprove the result
  • Build in small, verified increments
  • Good feedback names the symptom, where it happens, and what "fixed" looks like
  • Judge fit for the real user

✗ Traps

  • Letting the producer grade its own work
  • Feedback like "not quite right"; ask the testers for concrete examples instead
  • Assuming "all tests pass" means done. Tests only confirm what you described.
  • Tuning to one editor's taste without asking the actual readers

4.4 Delegation: which steps go to Claude

Decide step by step, not for the whole process

Break the process into steps and decide each one. Most workflows end up with some steps delegated and some kept.

Augment means adding Claude to steps that already exist. Redesign means replacing the structure, e.g. five teams passing claims by email become one Claude agent that triages, requests documents and routes each claim.

Diligence: ownership stays with you

You own the final result fully, whatever Claude contributed. Sign-off and vouching steps stay human. Write a pass/fail definition of done before you delegate.

4.5 Explaining value and limits to stakeholders

N K W S
  • Next-token prediction: where answers come from; "sophisticated autocomplete"
  • Knowledge: fixed at the training cutoff; internal facts must be supplied
  • Working memory: the context window, a fixed size; chunk, summarise or retrieve
  • Steerability: how closely it follows instructions

Each property runs from a zone where Claude is reliably strong to a zone where it slips. Explain a bad output by naming the property and where the task landed.

  • Different answers on different runs are expected, not a malfunction. Facts that differ between runs are the ones to verify.
  • Pitch it as a person working with Claude, not a replacement. "No more legal review" is wrong.
  • Justify a smaller model for simple, high-volume work (e.g. classifying invoices) on cost and speed.
  • Promise safeguards, not perfection. Report pilot results as they were, including where people still check the output.

  2. Switch to a more capable model

5.3 Writing project instructions

  • Write down the team's unwritten standards (house voice, audience) first, then put them in the instructions.

  • Set a role or perspective to shape expertise, tone and priorities.

✓ Do

  • Remove superseded files
  • Move a correction you keep repeating into the project instructions
  • Archive a finished project. That keeps its members, permissions and knowledge, and unarchiving restores everything.

✗ Myths

  • "A new chat fixes outdated answers." It loads the same stale knowledge.
  • "Claude prefers the newer file." Nothing marks the old one as superseded.
  • "A correction in chat carries over." Chats don't share context.
  • "Archiving removes access." Remove the member in the sharing settings instead.

In the project instructions. They apply to every chat in the project.

Governance, Risk and Responsible Use

6.1 Appropriate and inappropriate use cases

The recoverable, reviewable work testA use case fits Claude when its mistakes can be noticed and fixed before they reach anyone who'd be harmed. If an error would land before anyone could catch it, the use case is wrong, however good the prompt.

🚫 Prohibited, whatever the safeguards

  • Biological, chemical, radiological or nuclear weapons, even for an accredited lab or a "red team"
  • Sexual content involving minors, including fiction or role-play, and even with parental consent
  • Exploits or malware for unauthorised offensive use

Affiliation, stated intent, consent and fictional framing never lift these.

⚖️ Dual-use work

Context decides: authorisation, consent and disclosure. Professional verification only adjusts dual-use blocks. Prohibited uses stay blocked.

🤖 Agent autonomy

Grant autonomy where mistakes are cheap to undo, and require approval where they aren't (a sent message, a purchase, a changed record). Start computer use on background research or testing, and Claude in Chrome on research or form-filling.

High-risk domains: Claude informs and a qualified person decides, reviewing each output before it reaches the person affected. Spot-checking a sample afterwards isn't enough.

6.4 Ethical AI use

Diligence

Whoever acts on, sends or ships the output owns it fully. No responsibility moves to the tool or the vendor.

Disclosure

A customer chatbot shows a clear notice at the start of the session. A buried clause in the terms isn't enough, and the bot must never pose as a human.

Decisions about people

Credit and employment are high-risk, even after a human made the decision. A rejection email still needs review and disclosure.

Over-reliance

Skimming a draft isn't judgment. Evaluate before acting, and keep the final high-stakes calls yourself.

Read the outputsNext to their inputs, to find the real problem

  • Feedback is a signal, not a specification. "It misses the decisions" could be several different faults.
  • Quality dropped after someone edited the instructions? Restore the old wording and rerun the same task. Don't stack more instructions on top.
  • One good answer doesn't prove the new wording works, because outputs vary. Compare across several tasks.

7.3 Optimising workflows

Save usage

  • Send one complete, well-structured message, not fragments
  • Upload once to a Project; reused content benefits from caching, but caches expire after inactivity
  • Start fresh when a long chat costs more than it returns
  • Switch off tools and servers the task doesn't need
  • Usage depends on context, model, effort and features. Find out where it goes before changing habits.

Make fixes stick

  • Lines you repeat every time belong in the project instructions
  • Before sharing a personal workflow with colleagues, write the steps down and re-test them on real cases
  • Maintain instructions, and revisit them after model releases, because old workarounds become overhead
